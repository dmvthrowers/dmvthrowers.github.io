YO-YO GEAR

Gear & Maintenance

Bearings · String · Pads · Silicone · Lube · Mods · Polish

Keep your yo-yo playing like new. This page covers the parts inside a yo-yo, how they changed over time, what most players use today, and how to maintain them, with both dedicated yo-yo products and low-cost DIY options. Every fact links to a numbered source, including YoYoExpert forum threads, the YoYo Wiki, and our own Maintenance Guide.

Stuck? Bring your yo-yo to a meetup. We keep spare strings, response pads, and lube on hand.[1]

Today’s Standard Setup
  • Bearing: C size (¼″ × ½″ × ⅜″), the main size for competition yo-yos. Centering (concave) designs like the KonKave keep the string in the middle of the bearing.[21][2][46]
  • Response: 19mm “Slim” silicone pads, the most common size, about 1–1.1mm thick, or flowable silicone in the groove.[8][44][14]
  • String: 100% polyester, now preferred over cotton. Neon yellow and white are the most popular colors.[14][53][34]
  • Lube: optional. A drop of thin lube keeps a bearing quiet and protected; it does not clean it.[14][50]
Part 1

Beginner Gear Basics

Responsive vs. unresponsive

A responsive yo-yo comes back with a tug. An unresponsive yo-yo has a wider gap and low-friction pads, so it only comes back when you bind, wrapping extra string into the gap.[7] Many starter yo-yos can be set up either way and grow with you. The YoYo Wiki’s beginner guide recommends starting with 1A (one-handed string tricks), buying a 10-pack of string, and keeping a spare set of response pads.[14]

Shapes

  • Imperial (classic): narrow with a thin gap. Old-school tricks and looping.[17][15]
  • Modified: narrow and rounded; the most popular shape for two-handed looping (2A).[15]
  • Butterfly: a very wide gap for string tricks; usually loops poorly.[15]
  • Wing: evenly distributed weight for speed and stability.[17]
  • H-shape: weight pushed to the rims for stability and spin.[17][15]
  • Wall details like straight or stepped walls keep the string from rubbing and slowing the yo-yo.[18] See our Yo-Yo Science page for the physics.

String length and the slipknot

A good starting length reaches from the floor to your belly button.[19] Tie a slipknot at the end and wear it between the first and second knuckle of your middle finger. Always wear the slipknot before you throw.[1]

Materials

Plastic is cheap and tough; aluminum is denser and smoother; bimetal yo-yos add steel or brass rims for more spin.[16]

Part 2

Bearings

A ball bearing has an outer race, an inner race, and balls between them, usually eight, held in place by a cage. Thin shields and C-clips keep out dirt; many players remove the shields for easier cleaning.[4][2]

History

  • 1959 Jack Sauer puts a ball bearing in a “yo-yo doll,” the first known ball-bearing axle.
  • 1965 Milton Isaacson puts a ball bearing in a typical yo-yo.
  • 1984 SKF makes novelty ball-bearing yo-yos.
  • 1990 Tom Kuhn releases the SB-2, the first all-metal ball-bearing yo-yo.
  • 1991 Yomega releases the Raider.
  • 1997 Playmaxx’s ProYo Turbo Bumble Bee is the first bearing yo-yo with a replaceable response system.
  • 1999 Duncan’s first ball-bearing yo-yos, the Hardcore series.
  • 2003 Dif-e-Yo introduces the KonKave centering bearing.
  • 2005 YoYoFactory’s G5 ships with bearing-based hubstacks.

Timeline from the YoYo Wiki.[2]

Shapes and types

  • Flat: the basic stainless steel 8-ball bearing.[48]
  • Centering (concave): the KonKave (2003) has a curved outer race that keeps the string centered and away from the response. Others followed: YoYoFactory’s Center Trac (flat with flared edges), V-shaped designs, YoYoRecreation’s DS, grooved bearings, and the stepped CLYW × iYoYo Pixel.[2][46]
  • 10-ball: ten balls instead of eight for a smoother, quieter spin, with a break-in period. One Drop ships them standard.[2][47]
  • Ceramic: silicon nitride balls in a steel race; pricier and more sensitive to shock.[2]
  • Sealed and lubed: One Drop’s Greasebucket is silicone-sealed and packed with lube for a consistently responsive, no-maintenance feel.[49]

ABEC ratings describe manufacturing tolerances, but most players find they make no difference in yo-yo play.[2] New bearings usually need breaking in with a few long sleepers.[2] Bearing removal tools make swapping easier without scratching the bearing.[58]

Part 3

Cleaning a Bearing

Clean a bearing when it gets noisy, gritty, or too responsive. New bearings are often lubricated to prevent rust, which can make them responsive until cleaned.[3]

Safety First: Adults Handle Solvents

Most cleaning solvents are highly flammable and give off fumes. Wear eye protection, work in a well-ventilated space away from flames, and have an adult handle them.[3][24]

  1. Remove the bearing, then pop off the shields with a pin at the C-clip. Many players leave shields off afterward.[3]
  2. Soak it in solvent in a glass jar, swirling or turning it with a toothpick.[3]
  3. Blow it dry with compressed air, or spin it on a pencil as it dries, so no residue stays behind.[3]
  4. Add a drop of lube if you want it quieter or more responsive (see below).[3]

Which solvent?

  • Mineral spirits (paint thinner) and lighter fluid are common choices; many players use lighter fluid, which is gentler on plastics than acetone.[3][25]
  • Acetone is a strong solvent, but it can damage plastics and paint, and the YoYo Wiki warns it can promote rust.[3][25]
  • Avoid rubbing alcohol and water, which can rust a bearing.[3][27]
  • Lower-fume option: some players use citrus (orange) degreasers, as skateboarders do, though others worry about residue.[24]

Step-by-step guides with photos: YoYoExpert and How To Yo-yo.[22][23]

Part 4

Lube

Lube does not clean a bearing. It keeps it quiet and protected, and it is optional.[14] A dry bearing is the least responsive but noisier, wears faster, and can rust.[3]

  • Thick lube makes a yo-yo more responsive, good for beginners and looping.[51][52]
  • Thin lube maintains an unresponsive bearing and extends its life. Use one drop.[50][52]

History

Players once used 3-in-1 oil for a responsive yo-yo and trumpet valve oil for an unresponsive one. Dedicated yo-yo lubes followed from brands like YoYoJam.[3]

Dedicated products

YoYoJam Thick and Thin Lube[51], One Drop V4M (thin)[50], and YoYoFactory Performance Oil (Long Spin or Loop/Response).[52]

Low-cost DIY options

  • Sewing machine oil works as a thin lube; players report it performs as well as yo-yo lube.[26][27]
  • Skateboard bearing lube has a similar thickness to thin yo-yo lube and costs less per ounce.[27]
  • Trumpet valve oil comes in many thicknesses, but it can damage plastic.[27][3]
  • In a pinch, for more response: our guide suggests a thin smear of lip balm without water in the ingredients, or one tiny drop of light cooking oil. Use sparingly.[1]
Part 5

Response Pads and Silicone

The response system grabs the string so the yo-yo can wind back up. When pads wear out or fall out, the yo-yo slips and won’t return cleanly.[7][1]

History

  • Fixed axle Friction from the axle itself winds the string.
  • c. 1950 A Duncan employee proposes the raised starburst around the axle.
  • Playmaxx Reverse starbursts (grooves), then rubber Brake Pads that look like cork. Brake Pads became the basis of modern replaceable pads.
  • Late 1990s Friction stickers (patented in Britain in 1984) catch on with the rise of aluminum yo-yos.
  • YoYoJam Rubber O-rings in a groove around the axle, often shaved flush.
  • Silicone Flowable silicone and silicone pads take over for unresponsive play.

Timeline from the YoYo Wiki.[7][8] O-rings faded because black rings left string dirty and grew less reliable as gaps widened; silicone was more consistent.[32]

Pad sizes

  • 19mm “Large Slim” silicone pads are the most common size today. Central Bearing Company makes them with YoYoFactory; other brands, including Duncan, make compatible 19mm pads.[8][43][45]
  • Standard 19mm pads are about 1–1.1mm thick; One Drop’s are thinner, about 0.7–0.8mm.[44]
  • Other sizes include small pads for A-size bearing yo-yos, 21mm “Large Broad,” and the older .555 size.[8][33]

Replacing pads

  1. Unscrew the yo-yo and find the response groove around the bearing seat.[1]
  2. Clean the groove with a cotton swab to remove debris and old adhesive.[1]
  3. Press in a new pad, sticky side down, so it sits flush.[1]

DIY: flowable silicone

Flowable silicone is a windshield and glass sealant sold at auto parts and hardware stores. YoYoExpert sells a Permatex tube for yo-yos.[42]

  1. Fill the response groove with flowable silicone and smooth it out.[42]
  2. Wipe off the excess carefully.[42][30]
  3. Let it cure about 12–24 hours, longer when humid, before playing.[42][31]
  • Clear flowable vs. red RTV: players find clear flowable easier and less messy. Red high-temperature RTV is stickier, harder to apply, and has a strong vinegar smell.[29][30]
  • Less response: some players prefer Permatex Ultra Grey for a very unresponsive bind.[31]
  • Save the tube: push a screw into the nozzle before it dries, then pull it out next time.[29]
  • Other options: some players have used bathroom silicone caulk successfully.[28]
Part 6

String

Materials and history

  • Cotton: the traditional material, still best for beginners and responsive play.[5][54] Classic manufacturers long used cotton string.[20]
  • Slick / 50-50: cotton blended with polyester or nylon; more durable.[5]
  • 100% polyester: the choice of professionals today for smooth, unresponsive play.[53][14]
  • Nylon and rayon: soft, springy synthetics; rayon is very durable.[5][36]

Thickness: string is described by its strand count. Type 8 (4×2) is the common standard; types 6 (3×2) and 9 (3×3) are favored for unresponsive play.[5] Kitty String and others also sell “fat” string.[55]

Colors: neon yellow and white are the most popular because they are easy to see; lime green is a close second. Twisted colorways help you see string tension.[34][35][53]

Care

  • Replace string when it frays, gets dirty or stiff, or has a tight knot near the bearing.[1][5]
  • Store yo-yos with the string unwound to keep it springy.[5]
  • If the string twists up when slack, fix the tension: throw a UFO across your body to loosen or the other way to tighten.[6]

DIY: make your own string

String makers twist thread with a drill on a simple rig. The YoYoExpert mega-thread’s basic recipe:[36]

  1. Set two anchors 12 feet apart and wrap thread between them 6–8 times. More wraps make a thicker string.
  2. Hook the thread to a drill and spin it, letting it shorten about 10–15% (“reduction”) while keeping it taut.
  3. Double it over a center anchor placed at half the reduced length, still taut, and let it twist together. Reverse the drill to relieve torsion.
  4. Cut, knot, and tie it to length on your yo-yo.
  • Spun polyester: cheap, soft, many neon colors; wears out fairly fast.[36]
  • Trilobal polyester: lasts a very long time and whips well, but feels coarse.[36]
  • Textured nylon: very soft and springy; weaker for whips and slacks.[36]
  • Even colors: pre-twist each color before spinning them together.[37]
Part 7

Mods, Spacers, and Shims

Players have long modified stock yo-yos, and many mods became standard features later, like recessed response.[9]

  • Recessing: cutting a pocket so pads sit flush and don’t rub the string.[9][7]
  • Siliconing: filling an O-ring groove or recess with silicone.[9]
  • Bearing swaps: for example, adding a KonKave bearing.[9]
  • Shims: thin rings on each side of the bearing that widen the gap for less response. Cardboard or plastic shims are recommended because metal ones can get stuck.[10] 3D-printed shims are fragile, so don’t overtighten.[57]
  • Spacers: pieces that seat the bearing on the axle; swapping widths changes the gap.[11] Duncan’s Mod Spacers (2012) let players change a yo-yo’s response and body shape.[12]
  • Sanding: for a smoother “grind” surface; see below.[9][13]

Part 8

Cleaning, Polishing, and Dings

Cleaning the body

Remove the axle and bearing, then wipe the yo-yo with a soft cloth, a drop of mild dish soap, and warm water. Rinse with a damp cloth and let it air dry.[38] Monkeyfinger Buff brightens anodized finishes and leaves a slick surface for grinds.[56][38]

Know the damage

A ding is a dent you can feel, from hitting something hard. Pinpricks are tiny dings, often in clusters. A scuff is surface marking that usually doesn’t break the anodizing.[41]

Smoothing dings

  • You can smooth a ding, but you can’t restore anodized color without removing the finish.[39]
  • Players knock down sharp edges with a small file, even a nail file, then smooth with fine sandpaper.[39]
  • To fully strip and polish a metal yo-yo, one guide spins it on a drill and works through sandpaper from about 80 to 250 grit, then 0000 steel wool, scratch remover, and metal polish.[40]
  • Sanding at low speed on a drill gives a satin “grind” finish; lower grits leave fine lines.[13]
  • Or keep the war scars. Plenty of players do.[39]

Quick Troubleshooting

From our Maintenance Guide[1]
ProblemLikely causeFix
Slips or won’t returnResponse pad worn or fell outClean the groove and replace the pad
Slips or won’t returnBearing needs lubeAdd thick yo-yo lube
Won’t sleepString installed wrong, or a knot at the axleRe-install or replace the string
Won’t screw togetherStripped axle from over-tighteningTry a longer axle; tighten only until snug

Work through it in order: pads, then bearing lube, then string. Most problems fall into one of those three.[1] Download the full DMV Throwers Maintenance Guide.
